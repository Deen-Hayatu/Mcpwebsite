Has the Fight Against Galamsey Been Perpetually Lost?

By Mohammad Deen Hayatu

Illegal small-scale mining, galamsey, is no longer just an environmental crime; it has become a test of the Ghanaian state’s credibility. Each administration comes promising decisive action, and each leaves the country with rivers still poisoned and forests cut to the bone. President John Dramani Mahama’s return to power in January 2025 re-ignited this debate, partly because of a highly publicised “120-day social contract” and partly because of the perception that he personally promised to end galamsey in four months.

But what was actually said? What has been done? And are we any closer to an answer, or just circling the same drain?

What Mahama and the NDC Promised

It’s important to separate rhetoric from the record.

January 2025, VOA interview: Mahama pledged to “reset Ghana and deal with illegal mining,” promising new legislation to ban mining in forest reserves and near water bodies, and to clean polluted rivers. No deadline was given [1].

The 120-Day Social Contract: Published as a campaign and transition document, it explicitly promised within four months to ban illegal and new mining in forest reserves, roll out the Tree for Life and Blue Water Initiative, and begin converting degraded rivers into “ecological recovery hubs” [2].

Later clarification: By May 1, 2025, Mahama insisted he “never believed the fight against illegal mining could be won in just four months” [3].

The contract promised action within 120 days, not the end of galamsey altogether. Political opponents blurred the distinction, and much of the public internalised the harsher version: that he had pledged to fix the problem in four months.

Government Actions Since January 2025

A timeline of concrete steps shows activity on multiple fronts:

February 2025 – Task force led by Dr. Valerie Sawyer inaugurated to oversee the 120-day contract [2].

March 2025 – Tree for Life and Blue Water Initiative launched to restore forests and rivers [2].

March 28, 2025 – Parliament passed the GoldBod Bill, creating the Ghana Gold Board to formalise and control the gold trade [4].

May 1, 2025 – Gold trading restricted to GoldBod-licensed buyers; foreigners barred [5].

June 2025 – New rules introduced on importing excavators, with permits and tracking systems [6].

July 2025 – A security-backed national task force launched to tackle illegal mining and gold smuggling [5].

Aug–Sept 2025 – Enforcement raids and seizures reported in Ashanti and other regions, but illegal activity persisted [7].

On paper, the record is not thin. The question is whether these measures have shifted conditions on the ground.

Public Reactions

The policy moves sparked a wide range of responses.

Opposition voices: Richard Ahiagbah of the NPP called Mahama’s comments “demoralising” and accused the government of lacking commitment [8].

Civil society: Economists and activists warned that the government “fails to grasp the crisis,” pointing out that even without new licenses, illegal mining and pollution continue [3].

NDC communicators: Hamza Suhuyini rejected the claim that Mahama ever promised to “end galamsey in 120 days,” calling it misinformation [9].

Community leaders: Writers such as Abdulai Abdul-Razak lamented worsening pollution and accused the NDC of not distancing itself from officials allegedly tied to galamsey [8].

Traditional authorities: Chiefs and elders in mining areas accused taskforces of selective enforcement while rivers continued to die [7].

Across political divides, frustration is evident: plenty of noise, little change on the ground.

Are the Stated Goals Being Met?

Measured against the administration’s own commitments:

Ban on new/illegal mining in forest reserves: No new licenses have been issued since January, but reports of illegal operations in reserves persist. Outcome: partial.

Ecological recovery (Blue Water, Tree for Life): Announced, but no public data yet on river cleanups or hectares restored.

Prosecutions: Fewer than 40 convictions out of more than 800 cases in recent years were admitted by Parliament [10]. No new figures show a breakthrough under Mahama.

River quality: Still deteriorating. The River Bonsa’s pollution forced a water plant shutdown in January 2025 [1]. No signs of reversal yet.

Gold trade reform: GoldBod is operational and reported 55.7 tonnes exported in five months, suggesting some grip on formalisation [11].

Structural reforms are underway, but ecological recovery and law enforcement trail far behind.

The Four-Month Narrative

This remains politically charged.

The 120-day social contract exists and included specific deliverables.

It was not a pledge to end galamsey everywhere within that period, but that interpretation gained traction.

Mahama himself dismissed the four-month claim as unrealistic.

The story is therefore a mix of fact and distortion. But in politics, perception counts as much as record. Many Ghanaians believed it, and now judge him against it.

Policy Roadmap: What Next?

To move beyond slogans, three steps are essential:

Immediate (3–6 months): Enforce a ban in forest reserves and river buffer zones. Deploy drones and satellite imagery to publish monthly maps of illegal sites.
Metric: number of sites closed and prosecutions filed.

Medium term (1–2 years): Create a national water-quality dashboard, tied to the Blue Water Initiative, with independent lab audits.
Metric: measurable reductions in turbidity and heavy metals in key rivers such as Pra, Offin, and Bonsa.

Long term (3–5 years): Formalise artisanal mining under strict environmental compliance, backed by GoldBod traceability.
Metric: percentage of gold exports certified as legal and environmentally compliant, alongside conviction rates for illegal syndicates.

Final Word

The fight is not yet lost, but it is slipping. The government has ticked the boxes of passing laws and forming taskforces, yet rivers still run brown with mercury and silt. Until prosecutions increase, rivers recover, and forests regrow, galamsey will remain proof of a state that talks tough but enforces weakly.
